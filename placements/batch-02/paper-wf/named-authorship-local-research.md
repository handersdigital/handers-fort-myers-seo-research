Local marketing advice often appears without a named researcher, source boundary, or correction path. That makes a polished article difficult to evaluate. Readers cannot tell who selected the evidence, what the author actually reviewed, or who will update an outdated claim.

## Attach a person to the method

A useful author record should identify the writer, current role, relevant scope, and the body of work connected to that person. It should not inflate credentials or turn a byline into a guarantee.

For local research, the method belongs beside the name. Was the claim observed on a public page, reported by the business, drawn from an official source, or inferred? When was it checked? What could not be verified? Those notes help a reader separate documented facts from interpretation.

## Give the work a correction path

Every research page should have a canonical URL, publication or review date, and a way to report a correction. When business information, platform behavior, or local conditions change, the author can revise the record instead of leaving an anonymous fragment online.

Named authorship also connects related work. A reader examining website structure, local SEO, advertising, or lead response can see whether the same person uses a consistent method across those subjects.

## Keep the claim narrower than the evidence

An author page should show responsibility, not manufacture authority. The strongest signal is a body of specific, inspectable work with clear boundaries.
