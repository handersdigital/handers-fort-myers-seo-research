A website can collect a valid inquiry and still lose it minutes later. The weak point is often the handoff: nobody owns the alert, the lead lands in the wrong inbox, or the customer receives a receipt with no useful next step.

## Document the full route

I would map one real inquiry through every system it touches. The record should name the page, form or phone number, required fields, notification recipient, customer confirmation, CRM destination, response owner, and fallback when the first person is unavailable.

Each step needs a test result. A “success” message in the browser proves only that the interface changed. It does not prove that the notification arrived, the contact was stored correctly, or a person was assigned.

## Write rules for normal and exceptional cases

Good automation handles routine work without pretending every lead is routine. It can acknowledge the inquiry, preserve source information, create the record, assign an owner, and prompt the next action. A person should still handle judgment, sensitive questions, unusual requests, and situations where the customer needs an immediate answer.

The fallback matters as much as the main route. What happens after a bounced email, failed integration, duplicate contact, missed call, or inquiry outside business hours? If the answer is unknown, record that gap before adding another tool.

## Measure the handoff after the submission

Useful checks include notification delivery, assignment time, first response, failed events, and whether source details survive. Those measures make the operating problem visible without promising that automation will create demand on its own.
